Every housing society already runs a management system. It is just not software.

There is a WhatsApp group with 180 members where notices go, and where four of them are arguing about parking. There is a file on the secretary's laptop called `Maintenance_2026_final_v3.xlsx`. There is a register at the gate where the guard writes visitor names in handwriting nobody can read, including the guard. There is a shoebox of receipts. And there is one person, usually the secretary, usually unpaid, usually tired, holding the rest of it together in their head.

That is a real system. It has a database, a notification service, a file store and an API. It also has exactly one server, no backup and no replica, which is why everything stops the week she goes to her daughter's wedding.

So let us design the software version. Slowly, in order, with numbers at every step — because the numbers are the part that decides the design, and they are the part everybody skips.

## How we are going to do this

Six steps, and the order matters:

1. Write down what it does.
2. Put numbers on it.
3. Draw the smallest thing that works.
4. Find what breaks first.
5. Decide what you would change, and at exactly what number.
6. Cost it.

Most people skip steps 2 and 4, draw step 3 with a message queue and three microservices in it because that is what the diagrams on the internet look like, and then spend nine months building a thing that two hundred families could have run on a laptop.

## Step 1 — what does it actually do

Write the list in plain words. No nouns you cannot explain to the secretary.

- **Maintenance bills.** Generate a bill for every flat each month. Apply late fees. Show a history.
- **Payments.** Let a resident pay online and get a receipt.
- **Visitor entry.** The guard logs a visitor at the gate. The resident gets a notification and approves or rejects.
- **Complaints.** Raise a ticket, assign it, close it.
- **Notices.** The committee posts something. Everybody sees it.
- **Documents.** Meeting minutes, audit reports, bye-laws. Uploaded rarely, read rarely.

Now the part people leave out: **how often does each one happen?** That single column changes the entire design.

And write down what it does **not** do, in the same place, with the same seriousness. No chat. No social feed. No marketplace. Not because those are bad, but because a scope you have not written down is a scope that grows every time somebody walks past your desk.

One request per second. Hold that in your head, because almost every instinct you have about architecture is wrong at that number.

Now storage, which is a separate question and usually the more interesting one:

<div className="sd-sum">
bills &nbsp;→&nbsp; 200 a month × 40 KB of PDF &nbsp;=&nbsp; 8 MB a month &nbsp;=&nbsp; <strong>96 MB a year</strong><br />
database rows &nbsp;→&nbsp; visitors, payments, tickets &nbsp;≈&nbsp; <strong>40 MB a year</strong><br />
visitor photos &nbsp;→&nbsp; 300 a day × 150 KB &nbsp;=&nbsp; 45 MB a day &nbsp;=&nbsp; <strong>16 GB a year</strong>
</div>

Look at the ratio. The photos are four hundred times everything else put together. The whole database for four years fits in a WhatsApp backup; the photos do not.

That is the kind of thing the arithmetic tells you and a diagram never will.

Three useful things fall out of that picture:

- **The peak is predictable.** You know the date. That is a gift — you can warm things up, or just spread the work out, which is free.
- **The floor is the real baseline.** Visitor entry never stops, so it sets the minimum size of everything.
- **Bill generation does not need to be fast.** Nobody is waiting on it. Doing 200 bills in one second and doing them over twenty minutes feel identical to a resident, and one of those needs a much bigger machine.

<div className="sd-note">
That last point is worth more than any technology choice on this page. A huge amount of what looks like a scaling problem is a batch job someone decided to run all at once for no reason. Spreading work out over time is the cheapest scaling technique there is, and it is usually a one-line change to a cron schedule.
</div>

No load balancer. No cache. No queue. No Kubernetes. One machine runs the app and the database, photos go to object storage because disks fill up and buckets do not, and a backup leaves the box every night.

The two things that are not optional, even here:

**The backup has to be restored at least once.** An untested backup is a feeling, not a backup. Restore it onto a spare machine, open the app, look at last month's bills. Then you know.

**Photos must never live on the server's disk.** This is the single cheapest future-proofing decision on the whole page. On day one, writing files to `/var/uploads` and writing them to a bucket are about the same amount of work. On the day you want a second server, one of those choices costs you a weekend and the other costs you nothing.

The number of flats in a society is fixed by concrete. Traffic is flat for the life of the building. What grows, relentlessly, is **stored bytes** — and the four things that will actually ruin your month are:

1. **Storage, which nobody is watching.** Visitor photos pile up at 16 GB a year and no one ever decides how long to keep them.
2. **The one box dying.** Not a scaling problem — an availability problem. The secretary calls you at 9pm on the 1st. Two hundred families cannot pay. You are the on-call rota.
3. **Notification fan-out.** The committee posts a notice and you need to push to 400 devices. Do it inline in the web request and the request times out. This is your first real queue, and it arrives for a reason that has nothing to do with scale.
4. **The backup you never restored.** See above. This is the one that ends societies' trust in software permanently.

Not one of those is fixed by a bigger server.

## Step 5 — if the size is fixed, stop here

This is the decision people get wrong in both directions, so here it is plainly.

**If the thing you are building serves a fixed number of people, do not build for scale.** One society is 200 flats today and 200 flats in 2040. Scalability is a cost you pay every day — in moving parts, in deploys, in the number of things that can be broken at 2am — and it buys you capacity you have mathematically proven you will never use. One box with a tested backup beats a clever distributed system that one person understands.

**If the number of customers grows, you are building a different product**, and four decisions get very expensive to reverse. Make them on day one, because at this size they are nearly free:

<div className="sd-sum">
1. &nbsp;a <code>society_id</code> on <strong>every single row</strong>, from the first migration<br />
2. &nbsp;files in object storage, never on the server's disk<br />
3. &nbsp;no state on the app server — no sessions, no uploads, no local cache<br />
4. &nbsp;anything slow goes through a queue, even if the queue is one database table
</div>

Each of those costs an afternoon now. Adding a tenant column to forty tables that already hold live money, in production, for fifty paying societies, costs considerably more than an afternoon.

Everything else — replicas, caches, shards, autoscaling — you add later, when a number tells you to. Which brings us to the numbers.

## Step 6 — fifty societies

Ten thousand flats. The arithmetic scales linearly, because societies are completely independent of each other:

<div className="sd-sum">
peak &nbsp;=&nbsp; 1 request/sec × 50 &nbsp;=&nbsp; <strong>~25 a second at the monthly peak</strong><br />
storage &nbsp;=&nbsp; 16 GB × 50 &nbsp;=&nbsp; <strong>800 GB a year</strong><br />
notices &nbsp;=&nbsp; one committee posts → <strong>10,000 pushes in a burst</strong>
</div>

Twenty-five requests a second is still, honestly, one server's work. But something else has changed: fifty societies are paying you, and "it is down, I am restarting it" is no longer a sentence you can say. **You scale out here for availability, not for load** — and knowing which of the two you are buying keeps you from overbuilding.

## Step 7 — five thousand societies

A million flats. Now it is a real system.

<div className="sd-sum">
peak &nbsp;=&nbsp; <strong>~2,500 requests a second</strong><br />
bill generation &nbsp;=&nbsp; 1,000,000 bills on the 1st. In one hour that is <strong>278 writes/sec</strong> on top of everything else. Spread over 24 hours it is <strong>12/sec</strong>.<br />
photos &nbsp;=&nbsp; 225 GB a <strong>day</strong>  →  <strong>82 TB a year</strong>
</div>

Read that middle line again, because it is the best illustration on this page of design mattering more than hardware. The *same* work, scheduled differently, is either a 23× write spike you must buy capacity for, or a rounding error. Nobody is sitting at their phone at 00:00 on the 1st waiting for a bill. Spread it out and the problem disappears for free.

Two things in that drawing are worth saying out loud.

**A retention policy is a scaling technique.** Keep every visitor photo forever and storage grows 82 TB a year, without limit, until it is the largest line on your bill by a wide margin. Keep them 90 days — which is longer than any security purpose requires — and storage settles at about 20 TB and *stays there forever*. No new technology. One configuration rule. It is the single highest-leverage decision in this entire post.

**Sharding is the last resort, not the plan.** A society management system is the most shardable thing imaginable: societies never read each other's data, so `society_id` is a perfect shard key and you will never need a cross-shard join. That makes it tempting. Resist anyway. One decent database primary with two read replicas handles a million flats comfortably, and a sharded system is permanently more expensive to operate, migrate and debug. Keep the shard key on every row so the door stays open, and walk through it only when a graph forces you to.

Two things in those tables deserve attention, and neither is a server.

**The biggest line at a million flats is text messages.** SMS is ₹1,50,000 — about 36% of the bill — while the entire app tier is ₹36,000, or 9%. Every engineering hour you spend shaving the app tier saves less than one product decision to prefer push over SMS and only fall back when a push genuinely fails. At scale the bill stops being an infrastructure problem and becomes a product problem.

**Storage would have been the biggest line without the retention rule.** Three years of keeping everything is 246 TB, which is about ₹4,92,000 a month — more than the rest of the system put together. The 90-day rule is worth more than every optimisation in this post combined, and you can ship it in an afternoon.

## The short version

- Write down what it does, then **write down how often each part happens**. The frequencies decide the design.
- Do the arithmetic before the drawing. A 200-flat society is about **one request a second**, and almost everything you have read about architecture is irrelevant at one request a second.
- **The average is a lie.** Look at the shape. Size for the 1st of the month, and then ask whether the spike even needs to be a spike.
- One box with a **tested** backup is a legitimate, grown-up design. The backup has to be restored at least once or it is not a backup.
- **If the number of users is fixed, do not buy scalability.** You pay for it daily and it buys capacity you have proven you will not use.
- **If it grows, spend one afternoon on the four things that are brutal to retrofit:** a tenant id on every row, files in object storage, no state on the app server, slow work behind a queue.
- Add everything else against a **written trigger**, in order: queue, then a second server, then a bigger server, then a cache, then a replica, then a retention rule — and only then, if a graph insists, a shard.
- Scaling out is usually bought for **availability**, not throughput. Know which one you are paying for.
- The cheapest wins are not technology. **Spreading a batch over 24 hours, and deciding how long to keep a photo**, each beat every server choice on this page.

System design is not the art of drawing impressive boxes. It is working out which number is going to move, how far, and doing the smallest thing that survives it.
